Groundwater Lithium & Dementia Prevalence in Texas

Co-authored with Tracy Dower  ·  Advised by Jacob Turner, PhD & Jacquelyn Cheun, PhD

Do Texas counties with higher, more consistent groundwater lithium concentrations show lower dementia prevalence? I test that question with county-level data and a homogeneity framework designed to examine sensitivity to heterogeneous county-level exposure measurements.

The Problem

Do Texas counties with more groundwater lithium have less dementia?

Lithium occurs naturally in groundwater at concentrations that vary widely across Texas counties. A growing body of epidemiological literature suggests low-dose lithium exposure may be associated with reduced risk of neurodegenerative disease, but evidence at the population level, and in Texas specifically, remains limited.

Research question: Do Texas counties with higher, more consistent groundwater lithium concentrations exhibit lower dementia prevalence, after accounting for small-sample constraints and exposure heterogeneity?

This project tests that question using observational county-level data and robust statistical methods appropriate for a small-sample ecological study.

The Data

460,137 well measurements compiled into a county-level dataset

Groundwater quality came from 460,137 Texas Water Development Board (TWDB) well measurements (1919–2019), compiled into a county-level dataset covering every water quality parameter. Dementia prevalence came from NORC Dementia DataHub county-level estimates for 2020. Texas county FIPS crosswalks and Census TIGER shapefiles supported the choropleth mapping.

The Approach

A homogeneity framework, then rank, model, and stress-test

Counties were classified using a three-criterion exposure homogeneity framework, at least 10 samples, at least 70% of wells in one tail of the statewide distribution, and a coefficient of variation ≤ 0.50, to identify counties where a county mean is a defensible proxy for population exposure. I then modeled the homogeneous subset with OLS and HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, supplemented with permutation testing, bootstrap confidence intervals, and Cook's D / leave-one-out diagnostics, and stress-tested the classification thresholds and sodium confounding.

Results

Model fit varied with the county subset and covariate adjustment

Across 240 counties with complete data, the unadjusted lithium model had R² = 0.009. The sodium-adjusted model had R² = 0.082 in the full sample and 0.226 in the 14-county homogeneous subset. Because both the sample and model specification affect fit, these differences do not establish that exposure filtering recovered a true effect. The subset lithium estimate remained uncertain (p = 0.73).

Key Insights

What the homogeneity framework actually revealed

01

The unadjusted full-sample association was weak

The unadjusted correlation was weak (r = −0.095). Exposure measurement error is one possible explanation, alongside confounding, limited signal, and other sources of uncertainty; this analysis does not distinguish them conclusively.

02

The subset estimate remained uncertain

In the sodium-adjusted 14-county model, the lithium coefficient was negative. It remained negative in 86% of leave-one-out fits and 66% of bootstrap samples, but the confidence interval included zero.

03

The coefficient sign depended on sodium adjustment

Within the homogeneous subset, the lithium coefficient was positive without sodium adjustment and negative after adjustment. This sensitivity limits confidence in a stable lithium association.

04

Maps provide descriptive context

The maps show regional variation in lithium concentrations and dementia prevalence. Visual overlap is descriptive and does not establish an individual exposure–outcome relationship.

Model Limitations

What this analysis should, and shouldn't, be trusted for

This is a small-sample ecological result, not a causal claim. At N = 14, the lithium coefficient’s p-value is 0.73 and the bootstrap confidence interval spans zero. These results are directionally consistent with the existing literature but are not statistically significant, and the ecological design does not support causal inference.
The methodological contribution is the framework itself. The framework could be adapted to other county-level exposure studies, but its thresholds and usefulness would need validation in each setting.
