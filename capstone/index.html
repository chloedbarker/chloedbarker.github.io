Groundwater Lithium & Dementia Prevalence in Texas

Co-authored with Tracy Dower  ·  Advised by Jacob Turner, PhD & Jacquelyn Cheun, PhD

Do Texas counties with higher, more consistent groundwater lithium concentrations show lower dementia prevalence? I test that question with county-level data and a homogeneity framework built to survive the measurement error a small ecological sample can't otherwise absorb.

The Problem

Do Texas counties with more groundwater lithium have less dementia?

Lithium occurs naturally in groundwater at concentrations that vary widely across Texas counties. A growing body of epidemiological literature suggests low-dose lithium exposure may be associated with reduced risk of neurodegenerative disease, but evidence at the population level, and in Texas specifically, remains limited.

Research question: Do Texas counties with higher, more consistent groundwater lithium concentrations exhibit lower dementia prevalence, after accounting for small-sample constraints and exposure heterogeneity?

This project tests that question using observational county-level data and robust statistical methods appropriate for a small-sample ecological study.

The Data

460,137 well measurements compiled into a county-level dataset

Groundwater quality came from 460,137 Texas Water Development Board (TWDB) well measurements (1919–2019), compiled into a county-level dataset covering every water quality parameter. Dementia prevalence came from NORC Dementia DataHub county-level estimates for 2020. Texas county FIPS crosswalks and Census TIGER shapefiles supported the choropleth mapping.

The Approach

A homogeneity framework, then rank, model, and stress-test

Counties were classified using a three-criterion exposure homogeneity framework, at least 10 samples, at least 70% of wells in one tail of the statewide distribution, and a coefficient of variation ≤ 0.50, to identify counties where a county mean is a defensible proxy for population exposure. I then modeled the homogeneous subset with OLS and HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, supplemented with permutation testing, bootstrap confidence intervals, and Cook's D / leave-one-out diagnostics, and stress-tested the classification thresholds and sodium confounding.

Results

Restricting to homogeneous-exposure counties tripled explanatory power

Across the 240 counties with complete data, groundwater lithium showed effectively no association with dementia prevalence (r = −0.095, R² = 0.009). Rather than treat this as the end of the analysis, I examined why the null appeared: a county-mean lithium value averages residents drawing from wells with substantially different concentrations, and this measurement error in the predictor attenuates regression coefficients toward zero regardless of the underlying effect.

Key Insights

What the homogeneity framework actually revealed

01

The full sample said nothing was there

Across all 240 counties with complete data, lithium and dementia showed essentially no relationship (r = −0.095, R² = 0.009), a result that measurement error alone can produce even when a real effect exists underneath it.

02

A homogeneity framework rescued the signal

Restricting to the 14 counties where a county mean is a defensible exposure proxy raised R² to 0.226. The lithium coefficient was negative, and 86% of leave-one-out slopes and 66% of bootstrap slopes stayed in the protective direction.

03

Sodium confounding alone flips the sign

Without adjusting for sodium, the lithium coefficient becomes positive. Controlling for geochemical co-variates turned out to be essential, not optional, in this analysis.

04

The geography lines up

Choropleth maps show lithium concentrated in the Panhandle and Trans-Pecos regions, which also show lower dementia prevalence, consistent with the regression result.

Model Limitations

What this analysis should, and shouldn't, be trusted for

This is a small-sample ecological result, not a causal claim. At N = 14, the primary model's p-value is 0.73 and the bootstrap confidence interval spans zero. These results are directionally consistent with the existing literature but are not statistically significant, and the ecological design does not support causal inference.
The methodological contribution is the framework itself. The homogeneity classification approach extends to other trace elements and health outcomes studied at the county level, independent of whether this particular lithium-dementia result replicates.
