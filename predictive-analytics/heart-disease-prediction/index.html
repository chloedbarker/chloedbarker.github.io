Heart Disease Prediction

Using routinely-collected patient survey data to identify individuals at elevated cardiovascular risk, and confronting what a model can and can't do when the outcome it's predicting is rare.

The Problem

Can survey-based patient information flag elevated cardiovascular risk?

Heart disease is the leading cause of death in the U.S., but it's also one of the most heavily studied conditions in medicine, and clinical risk scores already exist. The question here was narrower and more practical: using only what a population health survey already collects (no labs, no imaging), how much signal is actually there, and where does a simple, interpretable model start to lose to a more flexible one?

The Data

CDC BRFSS survey respondents

319,795 respondents from the CDC's Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, split 80/20 into training (255,837) and test (63,958) sets, stratified to preserve the class balance.

319,795 Survey respondents
18 Predictors (BMI, age, smoking, general health, sleep time, and more)
8.6% Prevalence of heart disease in the sample
91.4% No heart disease, the class imbalance every model had to contend with

Respondents with heart disease skewed visibly older and reported more days of poor physical health, an early signal of which predictors would carry the most weight.

The Approach

From an interpretable baseline to a tuned comparison

I started with an additive logistic regression for interpretation, then let GLMNET select a non-additive specification with polynomial and interaction terms, and benchmarked both against LDA, QDA, and kNN on held-out data.

EDA & Cleaning → Additive Logistic Model → GLMNET Feature Selection → Non-Additive Model → LDA / QDA / kNN Comparison → ROC / AUC Evaluation
Results

The non-additive logistic model generalized best

Adding polynomial and interaction terms to the logistic model improved discrimination meaningfully over the additive baseline, and both logistic specifications comfortably outperformed LDA, QDA, and kNN on AUROC.

Key Insights

What actually predicted risk

01

Age was the strongest single driver

Holding other predictors constant, respondents 80+ had roughly 25× the odds of heart disease of the 18–24 reference group (95% CI 20.7–30.8), a near-monotonic climb across every age band.

02

Self-rated general health carried outsized signal

Respondents reporting "poor" general health had 6.57× the odds of heart disease versus "excellent" (95% CI 6.01–7.18), a single subjective survey question doing real predictive work.

03

Stroke history nearly tripled the odds

A prior stroke was associated with 2.85× the odds of heart disease (95% CI 2.71–2.99), the strongest binary clinical-history predictor in the model.

04

Sex was a persistent, moderate factor

Men had roughly double the odds of women (95% CI 1.97–2.10) after adjusting for age, health status, and the other 15 predictors.

General health and stroke history ranked as the two strongest categorical predictors, consistent with the odds ratios above.

Model Limitations

Where this model should, and shouldn't, be trusted

Class imbalance held sensitivity low across every model. With only 8.6% of respondents reporting heart disease, models optimized for overall accuracy defaulted toward predicting "no disease," and LDA reached 88% specificity but caught only 31% of true positive cases. I chose not to tune this away with synthetic resampling, since doing so would trade a documented, interpretable trade-off for an artificial one.
Self-reported, cross-sectional survey data. BRFSS responses are subject to recall and social-desirability bias, and every predictor here is observational, so the odds ratios describe association, not causation, and shouldn't be read as a clinical risk calculator.
