Diabetes Transitional Care Resource Allocation System

Hospitals can't give every diabetic patient intensive post-discharge support. This tool ranks patients by 30-day readmission risk, plans cost and capacity scenarios, and grounds its guidance in CMS/AHRQ documents, so a care-management leader can decide who gets scarce transitional-care resources first.

The Problem

Who gets scarce transitional-care resources first?

A hospital can't fund enhanced follow-up for every discharged diabetes patient, staff hours and intervention slots are fixed. The real decision isn't "who might be readmitted," it's: given a fixed capacity, which patients does supporting actually pay off for, in both clinical and financial terms? That's a resource-allocation problem, not just a classification problem, so the model had to be evaluated on capacity-aware business metrics, not accuracy.

The Data

101,766 hospital encounters, narrowed to the real decision population

Built on the UCI Diabetes 130-Hospitals dataset. Expired and hospice discharges were excluded, since transitional care isn't a relevant decision for them, and a patient-grouped train/test split kept the same patient's encounters from leaking across train and test.

101,766 Total hospital encounters in the raw dataset
99,340 Encounters in the eligible decision population
500 Assumed transitional-care capacity (highest-risk patients)
3 Tiers in the resulting intervention ladder
The Approach

Rank, tier, ground, compare, then price it out

A tuned Gradient Boosting model scores every patient's 30-day readmission risk; those scores sort patients into a 3-tier intervention ladder, from routine discharge support up to intensive nurse care management. A RAG layer answers care-management questions by retrieving from actual CMS/AHRQ source documents rather than generating unsupported answers, and a separate fine-tuned text classifier is evaluated on the exact same business scorecard as a required comparison.

Clean & Group Features → Patient-Grouped Train/Test Split → Tune Gradient Boosting (PR-AUC) → Calibrate Probabilities → Compare vs. Fine-Tuned Classifier → RAG Guidance + Cost Planner

Every scored patient maps to one of three support levels, so the model's output is a staffing decision, not just a risk number.

Results

At the top 500 highest-risk patients, the model roughly triples random targeting

Evaluated where a hospital would actually operate, the top 500 highest-risk patients, the calibrated Gradient Boosting model beat a fine-tuned text classifier on every business metric, including going net-positive where the text model went net-negative.

Under the default cost assumptions ($15,000 per readmission, $300 per intervention, 10% intervention effectiveness), acting on the top 500 patients avoids an estimated 17.6 readmissions, $263,700 in gross avoided cost against $150,000 in intervention spend, for a net value of +$113,700 and a break-even effectiveness of just 5.7%. All of these assumptions are adjustable live in the app.

Key Insights

What the comparison actually showed

01

Accuracy was actively misleading here

The model hits 88.7% raw accuracy, but recall at a standard 0.5 threshold was under 1%, because readmission is rare. Ranking the top 500 and reporting precision/recall there is what a hospital can actually act on.

02

The fancier model lost to the simpler one

The fine-tuned text classifier undershot the tabular Gradient Boosting model on every business metric, including going net-negative in expected value, a reminder that matching the model to the deployment metric beats defaulting to whatever's newest.

03

Calibration made the dollar figures honest

Because the deployed model's probabilities are calibrated, its risk scores mean something as actual probabilities, not just a ranking, which is what makes the net-value and break-even calculator meaningful instead of decorative.

04

Guidance is grounded, not generated

The RAG layer retrieves from CMS/AHRQ source documents rather than letting a model freestyle a clinical recommendation, and every answer in the app is cited back to a source page.

Model Limitations

What this prototype should, and shouldn't, be trusted for

This is a prototype trained on public historical data, not validated against any specific hospital's own patient population. The readmission and intervention costs are adjustable scenario inputs, not measured financial outcomes, and predicted risk is meant to support human review, not replace clinical judgment.
Recall at capacity is still modest. Even the deployed model captures only about 1 in 11 true 30-day readmissions within the top 500 patients. Fairness and calibration would need ongoing monitoring before any real deployment, this is decision support, not a finished clinical tool.
