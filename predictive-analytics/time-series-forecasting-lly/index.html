Time Series Forecasting — Eli Lilly (LLY)

Forecasting Eli Lilly's monthly stock price at two different horizons for a healthcare investment fund, and learning that the model that wins at three months is not the model that wins at twelve.

R tswge ARIMA VAR MLP Neural Net Time Series

The Problem

Which model actually forecasts a volatile stock, and over what horizon?

A healthcare investment fund tracking Eli Lilly's GLP-1-driven run needed forecasts at two different horizons, three months and twelve months, and wanted to know whether one model could serve both or whether the horizon itself should drive the model choice.

The Data

136 months of LLY, plus two comparison stocks

Monthly log-adjusted closing prices for LLY from 2015 to 2026, split into 124 months of training data with the final 12 months held out for testing. Two explanatory series were tested alongside LLY's own history: XLV, the broad healthcare sector ETF, and NVO (Novo Nordisk), LLY's direct competitor in the GLP-1 drug market.

136 Months of price history modeled
124 Training months
12 Held-out test months
2 Comparison series tested (XLV sector ETF, NVO competitor)
The Approach

Five models, evaluated at two horizons

I log-transformed price to stabilize variance, confirmed the series was nonstationary (a single difference was enough to fix that), then fit five different models and evaluated each one on the same held-out 3-month and 12-month windows using average squared error (ASE).

Log-Transform & Stationarity Check → ARMA & ARIMA (Univariate) → VAR with XLV & NVO → MLP Neural Net → ASE Evaluation (3-mo / 12-mo)
Results

No single model won both horizons

ARIMA was the strongest short-term forecaster but degraded sharply at twelve months, while a nonlinear neural network reversed that pattern almost entirely.

Key Insights

What actually separated the models

01

ARIMA won short-term by riding the random walk

Stock prices behave close to a random walk, so ARIMA's short-horizon forecast is mostly an extension of the most recent trend, and that's exactly what worked at three months.

02

That same behavior sank it long-term

Projecting the recent trend forward for twelve months meant extrapolating LLY's explosive 2022-2024 GLP-1 run indefinitely, and the ASE blew out to 0.355 as a result.

03

A nonlinear model handled the long horizon better

The MLP neural network produced the lowest 12-month ASE, suggesting the longer-term pattern in LLY's price isn't purely linear the way ARIMA assumes.

04

The competitor mattered more than the sector

VAR with Novo Nordisk, LLY's direct GLP-1 rival, beat VAR with the broader healthcare ETF at both horizons, company-specific competition carried more signal than sector-wide movement.

Model Limitations

Where these forecasts should be trusted, and where not

Only 12 months were held out for testing. With a test window that short, the ASE estimates are sensitive to exactly which 12 months got held out, and LLY's 2022-2024 run was unusually volatile even for a stock, so these results may not generalize to a calmer market.
Monthly returns are close to white noise. The ARMA fit on differenced log returns showed very limited predictable structure, a reminder that no model here should be read as more precise than a stock forecast can honestly be. The wide prediction intervals at the 12-month horizon reflect that real uncertainty rather than a modeling failure.
