Hospital Length of Stay Prediction

Finding the predictors that most influence how long a patient stays in the hospital, so hospitals can target the factors most likely to reduce unnecessary patient days.

The Problem

What actually drives how long a patient stays?

Hospitals want to reduce unnecessary patient days, but that starts with knowing which factors actually move the needle. Objective one was building an interpretable model to identify those key predictors. Objective two was checking whether a more complex model, or a completely different approach like k-NN, could predict length of stay meaningfully better.

The Data

113 hospitals, several operational and clinical factors

Each hospital record includes infection risk, number of beds, average patient and nursing counts, percent of services offered, region, medical school affiliation, average patient age, and diagnostic testing rates, alongside its average length of stay.

113 Hospitals in the dataset
3 Models compared: Simple MLR, Complex MLR, k-NN

Length of stay was longest in the Northeast and shortest in the West, and ran higher at hospitals with a medical school affiliation.

The Approach

Start interpretable, then see if complexity earns its keep

I built a simple, additive regression first so every coefficient stays interpretable, then compared it against a complex regression with interaction and polynomial terms, and a k-NN model, to see whether the added flexibility actually improved predictions.

EDA & Correlation Analysis → Simple MLR → Complex MLR & k-NN → Model Comparison → Recommendation

Infection Risk was tied for the strongest correlation with length of stay of any single predictor, a signal that carried through to every model below.

Results

The complex model won, k-NN didn't

All metrics below are on the log-transformed length of stay scale used for modeling. The complex, interaction-and-polynomial regression beat the simple model on every metric, while k-NN, despite being the most flexible option, performed worst.

Key Insights

What the data actually showed

01

Infection Risk was the most consistent predictor

It was significant in every model tested, simple, complex, and as an interaction term, making it the clearest lever for hospitals looking to shorten stays.

02

The simple model was a solid, honest baseline

With clear coefficients and meaningful inference, the simple MLR explained 59.7% of variance on its own, before any interaction or polynomial terms were added.

03

Added complexity paid off here

Interaction and polynomial terms lifted adjusted R² from 0.597 to 0.669 and lowered every error metric, a substantial improvement for the added complexity.

04

More flexibility isn't automatically better

k-NN, the most flexible of the three approaches, had the worst error metrics of the three and none of the interpretability, a useful reminder that flexibility alone doesn't guarantee a better model.

Recommendation

Complex, non-additive MLR

The complex MLR is the recommended model. It kept most of the interpretability of a regression model while meaningfully outperforming both the simple baseline and k-NN on every error metric, making it the most useful option for a hospital trying to both predict and understand length of stay.
