Sale Price Prediction

Co-authored with Tracy Dower

Two questions on the same Ames, Iowa housing dataset: does the price you get per square foot depend on which neighborhood you're in, and separately, how accurately can sale price be predicted for a citywide Kaggle competition?

The Problem

Does price-per-square-foot depend on the neighborhood, and how well can price be predicted?

A realtor client wanted to know whether an extra 100 square feet of living area is worth the same amount everywhere in Ames, or whether that depends on the neighborhood. Separately, a Kaggle competition asked a broader question: build the most accurate sale price model possible, using whatever predictors help.

The Data

Ames, Iowa home sales

1,460 residential home sales with 79 recorded variables covering size, quality, condition, and location. The neighborhood question focused on three neighborhoods: NAmes, Edwards, and BrkSide.

1,460 Homes sold in Ames, Iowa
79 Recorded variables per home
3 Neighborhoods compared for the inference question
The Approach

Two questions, two ways of answering them

For the neighborhood question, I log-transformed price and living area to satisfy regression assumptions, then tested whether adding a neighborhood-by-area interaction improved the model. For the prediction question, I compared four candidate models of increasing complexity and validated the winner against standard regression diagnostics before submitting to Kaggle.

Log-Transform & Clean → Test Neighborhood Interaction → Compare Candidate Models → Validate Assumptions → Kaggle Submission
Results, Part 1

Yes, the price-per-square-foot effect depends on the neighborhood

The interaction between living area and neighborhood was statistically significant (F = 8.649, p = 0.0002), meaning an extra 100 square feet is not worth the same amount everywhere.

8.649 F-statistic for the neighborhood interaction
0.0002 p-value, well below the 0.05 threshold
~75% Median price increase in BrkSide from doubling living area
~38% Same increase in NAmes and Edwards, the reference slope

BrkSide homes start from a lower base price but climb faster with size, crossing above NAmes and Edwards by around 300 additional square feet. Edwards tracked NAmes closely enough that a separate slope wasn't statistically justified (p = 0.52).

Results, Part 2

The full prediction model explained 86.65% of price variance

Adding overall quality, garage area, basement size, and neighborhood on top of living area moved the model from a rough baseline to a strong predictor, and it held up on Kaggle's held-out test set.

86.65% Variance in log sale price explained by the final model
0.150 Kaggle RMSLE, down from 0.229 for the simplest model

Key Insights

What actually drove price

01

Overall quality was the single strongest predictor

Of every variable tested on its own, a home's overall quality rating explained the most variance in price, ahead of size, location, or age.

02

Location ranked second, ahead of most physical features

Neighborhood alone outranked garage size, kitchen quality, and bathroom count as a standalone predictor of price.

03

BrkSide homes start cheaper but catch up fast

BrkSide's baseline price was significantly lower than NAmes (p = 0.001), but its steeper slope meant BrkSide homes overtook NAmes and Edwards in price once living area got large enough.

04

The full model roughly halved the prediction error

Going from a one-variable baseline to the final model dropped the Kaggle score from 0.229 to 0.150 RMSLE, and pushed explained variance from 66.8% to 86.65%.

Model Limitations

What the model can and can't claim

This is an observational dataset. The neighborhood findings describe association, not causation, since homes weren't randomly assigned to neighborhoods. Differences in price-per-square-foot could reflect factors correlated with neighborhood, like lot size or school zoning, rather than location itself.
Diagnostics were mostly clean. The Durbin-Watson statistic (2.003, p = 0.94) showed no meaningful autocorrelation, and every home's Cook's D stayed well below 1, so no single sale distorted the model. The main soft spot was a longer left tail in the error distribution and two homes with somewhat higher leverage, though neither had an outsized residual.
