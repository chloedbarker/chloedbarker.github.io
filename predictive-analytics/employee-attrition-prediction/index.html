Employee Attrition Prediction — Frito-Lay

Flagging which Frito-Lay employees are at risk of leaving, and choosing the decision threshold around what a missed departure actually costs instead of chasing accuracy for its own sake.

The Problem

Which employees are likely to leave, and what should the company do about it?

Frito-Lay wanted to flag employees at risk of attrition before they left. But a plain accuracy-optimized model treats a missed departure and a false alarm as equally costly, and they aren't even close: replacing a departing employee runs 50%-400% of their salary, while a retention incentive costs about $200. The real question wasn't just who's likely to leave, it was what decision threshold makes financial sense given how lopsided those two costs are.

The Data

870 employees, and a real income gap between who stays and who leaves

Of 870 employees, 730 stayed and 140 left, an attrition rate of about 16%. Before building any predictive model, I tested whether monthly income actually differed between the two groups, and it did.

870 Employees in the dataset
16.1% Attrition rate (140 of 870 left)
$6,702 Avg. monthly income, employees who stayed
$4,765 Avg. monthly income, employees who left

A two-sample t-test rejected the null of equal means (t = -5.32, p < .001): employees who left earned $1,220 to $2,654 less per month on average, at 95% confidence.

The Approach

Cost first, model second

Rather than optimize for accuracy, I set the decision threshold using the actual dollar costs on each side, a $200 retention incentive against a replacement cost of 50%-400% of the average $76,683 salary, then compared a Naive Bayes model against a kNN model at their respective cost-optimal thresholds.

EDA & Cost Framework → Naive Bayes (threshold 0.12) → kNN, k = 3 (threshold 0.15) → Cost-Based Comparison → Recommendation
Results

kNN cut the projected cost range by more than half

Both models identified Age, Job Role, and Overtime as the strongest predictors. But at their respective cost-optimal thresholds, kNN traded some specificity for a large jump in sensitivity, and that trade paid off financially.

Key Insights

What actually mattered

01

Missing a leaver is far more expensive than a false alarm

A false alarm costs about $200, missing someone who actually leaves costs 50%-400% of their $76,683 salary. That asymmetry should drive the decision threshold, not overall accuracy.

02

Age, job role, and overtime held up across both models

The same three predictors surfaced as the strongest signal in both the Naive Bayes model and the kNN model, model choice didn't change what was actually driving attrition risk.

03

Income really is different between the two groups

A t-test confirmed employees who stayed earned significantly more per month than those who left, evidence that monthly income is worth watching even outside the final model.

04

A less "accurate" model was the better business decision

kNN's 72.76% accuracy wasn't the highest it could have been tuned for, but a threshold chosen for cost rather than accuracy cut the projected replacement-cost range by more than half versus Naive Bayes.

Model Limitations

What these numbers can and can't tell you

The cost figures rely on a wide assumed range. Replacement cost is modeled at 50%-400% of salary, a range drawn from general HR literature rather than Frito-Lay's own accounting, so the dollar figures should be read as a plausible range, not a precise budget number.
This is observational data from one company at one point in time. These predictors and thresholds reflect Frito-Lay's workforce specifically and may not transfer directly to a different employer or industry.
