Clustering & Association Rule Mining

Two very different unsupervised learning questions on two very different datasets: does a synthetic health dataset actually contain hidden patient segments, and which products at an online retailer keep showing up in the same basket?

The Problem

Does this data actually cluster, and can it predict what sells together?

Two datasets, two different unsupervised questions. On the health dataset: do six different clustering algorithms agree that meaningful patient segments exist, and do those segments line up with actual disease risk? On the retail dataset: which products get bought together often enough, and reliably enough, to be worth acting on?

The Data

100K synthetic health records, 541K real retail transactions

The health dataset is fully synthetic (Kaggle, CC0), built for algorithm testing rather than medical interpretation. The retail dataset is real UK online-retailer transaction data (UCI, CC BY 4.0) spanning thousands of customers and products.

100,000 Health & lifestyle records, 16 variables
541,909 Retail transactions, ~4,372 customers
6 Clustering algorithms benchmarked
~4,070 Distinct retail items mined for associations
The Approach

Six clustering methods, evaluated the same way

For clustering: scale and encode the features, then run K-Means, Hierarchical (Ward and Average linkage), Gaussian Mixture Models, DBSCAN, Spectral, and graph-based clustering across a range of k, scored on both internal metrics (how tight the clusters are) and external metrics (whether they recover the actual disease-risk label). For the retail data: mine frequent itemsets with Apriori and FP-Growth at different support thresholds, then generate and rank association rules.

Scale & Encode → 6 Clustering Methods (k = 2-8) → Internal + External Validation → Apriori / FP-Growth → Rule Ranking
Results

The health data doesn't cluster. The retail data clearly does.

Every clustering method independently converged on the same answer: at best a very weak two-way split, with essentially zero agreement with the true disease-risk label. The retail basket data told the opposite story, producing strong, actionable rules.

0.000 Adjusted Rand Index, best clustering method vs. true disease risk
24× Lift on the strongest 3-item retail association rule

Key Insights

What the two datasets actually showed

01

Six algorithms agreeing is a real result

K-Means, HAC, GMM, DBSCAN, Spectral, and graph-based clustering all independently landed on the same weak, at-best-two-cluster structure, that convergence is stronger evidence than any single method's score.

02

DBSCAN's high silhouette score was misleading

DBSCAN hit a silhouette of ~0.56 at one setting, but only because nearly every point was labeled noise around a few tiny core clusters, a reminder that a single metric can look good for the wrong reason.

03

A specific product trio dominated the retail rules

Three stock codes (22697, 22698, 22699) showed up together with 24× lift and high confidence, a clear, actionable signal for bundling or merchandising.

04

The support threshold controlled everything

Dropping the minimum support from 2% to 3% collapsed 251 itemsets down to 94, all singletons, and eliminated every association rule, interpretability lived entirely in that one parameter.

Limitations

What not to take from this

The health dataset is fully synthetic. It's built for testing clustering algorithms, not for real epidemiological insight, the near-zero correlations that produced weak clusters reflect how the data was generated, not a real population.
Association rules can reflect popularity, not real association. Some high-confidence rules had a lift of about 1, meaning the items just happen to be popular individually rather than actually bought together for a reason, those were filtered out rather than reported as findings.
